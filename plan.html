01 — Brief

What the site has to do

Someone hears about LvlUp, searches the venue name or "PS5 gaming near me", and lands here. They need four answers fast: what stations you have, what an hour costs, where you are, and how to hold a slot. Everything else is secondary.

So the site is built around one action — book a station — and around local search, because a lounge only sells to people within a few kilometres. But hourly walk-ins alone do not pay rent. The plan below also chases the two revenue lines that actually carry a lounge: group bookings (one birthday party is worth forty walk-in hours) and repeat regulars (passes, tournament nights, the WhatsApp list).

02 — Direction

How it should look and feel

The obvious move for a gaming brand is neon purple, RGB glow and a black page. Every gaming café in the country already did it, and it reads cheap. LvlUp goes the other way: the room at night. Deep charcoal, one warm light source, everything else quiet. Closer to a listening bar than an internet café — which is also what justifies charging more than the shop down the road.

Room#121315 Page ground. Off-black, never pure #000.
Surface#1B1D20 Raised panels, sticky booking bar.
Lamp#C8763A The single accent. Buttons, prices, live badges.
Paper#E8E3DC Headlines and body. Warm white, not stark.
Dim#8A8781 Secondary text, captions, labels.
Typefaces
Satoshi (display) · Geist Mono (data) Every price, hour, station number and countdown sets in mono — an arcade-cabinet cue that also makes the rate card scannable. Headlines stay tight and mid-sized rather than screaming. Not Inter, not a gamer display face. Self-hosted through next/font so there is no render-blocking font request.
Layout
Asymmetric split hero, bento station grid, horizontal games shelf Copy and booking button hard left, the room photograph bleeding off the right edge. Stations as a bento of unequal tiles, not three identical cards. Games as a drag-scroll shelf, the way you would actually look along a rack. No centred hero over a dark image. No three-equal-cards feature row. Both collapse to a single column below 768px.
Motion
Framer Motion, spring physics, restrained Staggered reveals on the station grid, a magnetic pull on the primary booking button, a live "open now" dot that breathes. All transform and opacity only. No custom cursor, no scroll hijack, no parallax on mobile. Everything respects prefers-reduced-motion.
Glass
CSS refraction, not a glass library The sticky booking bar and price chips use backdrop-filter plus a 1px inner highlight and a tinted inner shadow. Reads as physical glass, costs nothing.

03 — Stack

Technical decisions

Framework
Next.js 15, App Router, TypeScript, React Server Components Content pages ship as static HTML with zero client JavaScript. Only leaf components that actually move — booking bar, games shelf, form — carry 'use client'. Instead of plain HTML (dies the moment you want booking) or a Vite SPA (weak local SEO).
Components
shadcn/ui, re-skinned Copied into the repo, not installed as a dependency, so the radii, borders and focus rings get rewritten to the charcoal-and-lamp palette. Used for the form, dialog, sheet, tabs and accordion — the parts where accessibility is easy to get wrong. Never in default zinc-and-rounded-lg state. A stock shadcn page is recognisable at a glance and undoes the premium argument.
Styling
Tailwind CSS v4, tokens in one file The five palette values above are the only colours in the project. Grid over flex-math for every multi-column layout.
Content
Typed data files in /content pricing.ts, stations.ts, games.ts, hours.ts, areas.ts. Changing a rate is a one-line edit. The same files feed the pages, the structured data and the SEO page generation. Instead of a CMS — overkill for a site you edit a few times a year.
Booking
WhatsApp deep link, primary. Server-action form as fallback. In India the booking conversation already happens on WhatsApp. The button prefills date, station and duration into the chat; you confirm in one reply. Every click fires a tracked event so you can see which page and which section actually produces bookings. Instead of real-time slot booking — that needs staff keeping a calendar honest all day. Revisit only when walk-in volume forces it.
Form handling
Next.js server action, mail via Resend No third-party embed, no client-side API key. Honeypot field and per-IP rate limit so bots do not fill your inbox. Group-booking enquiries route to a separate subject line — those are the high-value leads.
Analytics
Vercel Analytics plus one custom event set Four events: whatsapp_click, call_click, directions_click, form_submit, each tagged with the page and section. Cookie-free, roughly 1KB, no consent banner needed. Instead of full GA4 — heavier, slower, and needs a cookie notice for a site this small.
Hosting
Vercel, custom domain, Mumbai edge region Free at this traffic, HTTPS and image optimisation included, deploys on git push. Serving from the nearest region shaves real milliseconds for Indian visitors.

06 — The 3D question

Verdict on the reference libraries

You sent three references. They are not equally safe on a site whose visitors are mostly on a mid-range Android over 4G, where every extra second of load costs bookings. Each was judged on one question: does it earn its weight?

shadergradient

In, quarantined

Used for exactly one thing: a slow-moving light wash behind the hero, standing in for the glow of screens in a dark room. It is the single most premium-feeling element on the site and it costs the most, so it is fenced in hard — loaded through next/dynamic with ssr: false, mounted only after the page is interactive and idle, and only when the viewport is 768px or wider, the connection reports 4g, the device reports 4GB or more of memory, and reduced-motion is off. Everyone else gets a static WebP frame exported from the same shader, so the art is identical and the largest paint is always just an image.

cost: three + @react-three/fiber + three-stdlib + camera-controls ≈ 160KB gzip · never on the critical path · never on mobile

React Three Fiber

In, as the host only

Arrives as shadergradient's dependency, so it is already paid for. The examples gallery informs the hero composition and nothing else. No 3D station cards, no scroll-linked scene, no model loading — a spinning PS5 model would add megabytes to sell a product the visitor already knows what it looks like.

cost: shared with shadergradient · same lazy gate

liquid-glass-js

Out — look kept, library dropped

It is genuinely beautiful and genuinely wrong for this site. It is a vanilla script-tag library, not a React component, so it fights Server Components. It requires WebGL 2 and it drives refraction by rasterising the page with html2canvas — screenshotting the DOM behind every glass element, repeatedly, on a phone. That is a frame-rate collapse on the exact devices your customers hold, and a Safari-14-and-up floor on top.

The effect it sells is reproduced in CSS for free: backdrop-filter: blur(18px) saturate(140%), a 1px inner highlight at 10% white, and an inset shadow tinted to the charcoal ground. Applied to the sticky booking bar, the price chips and the nav. Same read, no WebGL, works everywhere.

cost avoided: html2canvas ≈ 48KB gzip plus continuous GPU repaint

shadcn/ui

In

Copied into the repo and re-skinned to the palette above. Accessible primitives for form, dialog, sheet, tabs and accordion — focus rings, keyboard traps and ARIA handled properly rather than reinvented. Only the components actually used get copied, so nothing unused ships.

cost: only what is imported · no runtime dependency

08 — Review loop

Three rounds, and what each one broke

The v1 plan was reviewed three times, each round against a different lens, each round rewriting the plan before the next one started. The gaps below are real defects that were in the plan you first saw.

ROUND 01

Commercial

Nothing measured whether the site sold anything.

Four tracked events on WhatsApp, call, directions and form, each tagged with page and section, so you can see which part of which page produces bookings and cut the rest.

The highest-value product was buried on page two.

Parties and lounge buyouts get their own block on the home page and a dedicated enquiry route. One birthday booking is worth roughly forty walk-in hours.

No answer to the empty weekday afternoon.

Off-peak pricing added as a first-class rate column and surfaced on the home page. Selling a dead chair at a discount is pure margin.

Nothing brought anyone back a second time.

Passes promoted with the per-hour saving shown, tournament dates given real event markup, and an opt-in on the booking form for the WhatsApp announcements list.

A first-time visitor had no reason to trust the place.

Live rating and real reviews with names, an explicit what-you-get list, and house rules stated up front so nobody arrives to a surprise.

ROUND 02

Marketing & SEO

Four pages is almost no surface to rank on.

Three to five hand-written area pages and eight game pages, each carrying content that is genuinely different. Enough surface to catch neighbourhood and title searches, small enough to stay honest.

Structured data stopped at the business listing.

Added Offer for passes, Event for tournament nights, FAQPage for the rules and AggregateRating for reviews, all generated from the same typed content files so they can never drift from the visible page.

Ignored the channel that actually decides local ranking.

Site treated as feeder to the Google Business Profile: name, address and hours match it character for character, and an exit QR code points at the review form. For a venue, the map listing outranks the website.

Links get shared on WhatsApp with no preview.

Per-page Open Graph images generated at build time — the rate card page previews as the rate card. In a market where sharing happens in group chats, that image is the advertisement.

Instagram traffic had nowhere to land.

A link-in-bio route with UTM tags that pushes straight to booking, so reel traffic is attributable instead of anonymous.

ROUND 03

Speed & premium

"Premium" and "fast" were asserted, never reconciled.

A written budget with numbers and an enforcing mechanism per row, checked in CI. The 3D moment is allowed only because it is architecturally incapable of touching the largest paint.

One reference library would have wrecked mobile.

liquid-glass-js rejected on inspection — it rasterises the DOM with html2canvas every frame and needs WebGL 2. The look rebuilt in CSS instead, at zero cost and with wider support.

No visual identity, only a component list.

A committed direction: the room at night, one warm accent, mono numerals, asymmetric layout. Specific enough to reject work that does not fit, which is what a direction is for.

Default component styling would have undone the premium claim.

shadcn/ui re-skinned before use — own radii, own borders, own focus rings. A stock install is recognisable on sight.

Accessibility was never mentioned, which on a dark site is where it breaks.

Contrast checked against the charcoal ground, visible focus states on every control, motion gated on prefers-reduced-motion, and the whole booking flow completable by keyboard.

Verdict · round 3 · ready to build

The plan is done. The content is not.

Nothing else is worth adding on paper — a fourth round would be polish on a document instead of progress on a site. Phases 1 through 4 can start today against placeholders.

Two things still decide whether this works, and neither is engineering. Photographs: the entire premium argument rests on the room looking as good as the site says it does, and no amount of shader work rescues bad photos of a good lounge. The real rate card: pricing is the page people screenshot, and it has to be exact before launch. Both are on you, and they are the only reason this plan cannot reach production.

The scores that sit at 8 rather than 9 stay there deliberately — marketing and premium are proven by traffic and by how the finished thing feels, not by a document claiming them.
